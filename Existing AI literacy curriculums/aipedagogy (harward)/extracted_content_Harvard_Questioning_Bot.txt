Questioning the Bot: Socratic Chats
with AI
By Nina Yuen
What happens after the prompt? Question AI-generated text using Socratic methods
and infuse moments of AI-free critique into evaluative writing.

https://aipedagogy.org/assignment/questioning-the-bot-socratic-chats-withai-2/

Learning Objectives
Critically deconstruct essays to assess strengths and weaknesses in content and
structure
Refine thinking through collaborative inquiry and role-play
Apply standards of strong writing and nuanced thinking to the development of voice
Respond to a chatbot with clarity, perspective, and human insight
Maintain authorial agency while engaging AI as a co-writer or collaborator

Instructions
Assignment Overview
Prompting is an important emerging skill in the context of AI–but what happens after? This
assignment begins where the chatbot output ends. Instead of competing with the AI,
students will be challenged to recognize its strengths, critique its weaknesses, and infuse
the generated material with their own voice and vision.
Students will engage the chatbot in a Socratic dialogue, posing questions, identifying
assumptions, and pushing past surface-level responses. To counteract any sense of
passivity or dependence on the tool, the assignment also includes “unplugged” moments for

AI-free writing and revision.
Students will move through a cycle of writing, prompting, analyzing, and revising—both
with and without AI—in order to compare writing approaches and deepen critical
engagement. They’ll start by brainstorming with pen and paper, then prompt the chatbot to
write sample essays with links to scholarly sources. They will make edits to the sample
essay and track changes, then prompt the chatbot to write a rubric that holds this first
essay to a new standard.

Assignment Structure
1. AI-free writing
Begin by choosing an essay topic that is appropriate to your classroom. Students
should be familiar with the topic, or at least have some background knowledge of the
subject area. For instance, an English class might choose the theme “Representations
of Race in 20th Century American Literature.” Students will write a short, in-class
essay using only human-generated sources and distraction-free tools (e.g., pen and
paper or a simple word processor). Keeping the essay relatively short allows for
quicker revision and ensures compatibility with chatbot input/output word limits.
2. Prompt the chatbot
Using the same topic, students will now engage with a chatbot (e.g., ChatGPT or
Claude). The chatbot can help brainstorm research questions, simplify complex texts,
map out research plans, suggest sources, or assist in summarizing findings. Students
should iteratively prompt the AI, experimenting and adjusting their inputs as needed.
Encourage them to document each step of the process and reflect on how the
chatbot’s responses influence their thinking.
3. Evaluate
Once students have completed their final essays, they will evaluate their work using
two approaches—first with AI, then without.
Start by using the chatbot to co-create a rubric for evaluation. As students apply
this rubric, the chatbot can help them organize their ideas, identify patterns, and
reflect on the distinctiveness of their voice.
Next, have students evaluate their essays without AI support. Ask students to
critically assess their work using personal or peer-developed criteria. Encourage

them to consider the limitations of the AI-generated rubric and push their analysis
further, adding their own insights to go beyond what the AI offered.
4. Discuss
When you are engaging in a Socratic dialogue with the chatbot, reflect with curiosity;
ask questions that open up chance, surprise, and discovery; cite expert sources; and
embrace ambiguity and complexity. Push the AI with follow-up questions like “Why?”,
“How?”, and “What if?” to challenge its assumptions and deepen the exchange. Don’t
accept its answers at face value. For inspiration, a Socratic dialogue might look like
this: Student: “Why do you think the author’s portrayal of race is effective?” AI:
“Because it challenges stereotypes.” Student: “What stereotype specifically? And how
does the text challenge it?” Aim for at least 3–5 layers of follow-up in your exchanges.
5. Re-writing with AI
Now that you’ve written one essay on your own, generated one with AI, and evaluated
both, it’s time to rewrite your essay—first with the chatbot’s help, then independently.
You’ll have access to the chatbot-generated essay transcript as a reference point to
help you reflect, revise, and make your writing more distinct and thoughtful. Let the AI
model suggest edits or rewrites, but treat these as prompts for further thinking rather
than final drafts.
First, prompt the chatbot to revise the original essay.
Then, use distraction-free tools to write another human-only draft.
Compare these versions and continue refining as needed, drawing from both
processes to enhance clarity, structure, and voice.
6. Compare
Now bring in both peer and AI perspectives. Ask a classmate and the chatbot to review
and compare the human-written and chatbot-revised essays. To prompt the chatbot for
comparison, try: “Which essay demonstrates stronger rhetorical strategies?” or “Which
essay more clearly expresses an original voice?” Use the rubric from earlier or
generate a new one together. Look for interpretive claims, rhetorical strategies, and
expressions of voice. What patterns or differences emerge across drafts?
7. Revise
The presence of the chatbot’s voice should prompt students to assert their own.
Encourage deeper engagement with sources, clearer articulation of ideas, and a

strong sense of personal authorship. Use this stage to synthesize everything learned
so far about AI, revision, and your own writing process.

Reflection
The following questions are designed to guide student thinking throughout the process and
support end-of-assignment reflection:
How did the essay change when you prompted the chatbot to a) respond from
different political perspectives b) write in the voice of a specific essayist c) modify the
essay for different audiences?
How might writers from the past have undergone a similar revision process?
When did the chatbot underperform–or overperform–in responding to your prompts?
What did the chatbot miss? What did it get right? How do your observations relate to
what you’ve learned about how LLMs work? Offer a theory about why it did well at
certain tasks and poorly at others.
Who did you choose to “speak” with in your chatbot dialogue? A
scholar/mentor/elder/fictional character/historic figure? How did the nature of your
learning shift depending on whom or what you were talking to?
What dialogue prompting tactics did you use? How were they different from the
framing of your in-person conversation?
Did any platform guardrails or institutional constraints appear? (e.g., “I am not able to
give advice,” or “I have to direct you to our office of communications.”)
At what stages did the chatbot enhance your learning? Where did it fall short or create
confusion?
How do you think your essay would have turned out differently if you had completed it
without a chatbot?
Do you relate to your words differently than you would if you had written them “from
scratch”?
Can you find places where you pivoted away from the chatbot’s assumptions or
questioned its framing?
In what ways did your personal style and voice diverge from the chatbot’s tone?

Did anything surprise you when you compared the essays? What questions, thoughts,
musings, or curiosities popped up for you?
What did you learn about yourself, your writing process, and the subject of your
essay?
What would be the implications for human writing if everyone co-wrote with chatbots?
If you were to share a piece of advice to fellow students about co-writing with
chatbots, what would you say?
When might using chatbots jeopardize the trust between you and your teachers?
Between you and your audience?
