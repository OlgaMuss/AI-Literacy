## 3.2 What we mean by “assessing a curriculum”

A curriculum can be assessed in two different senses, and we do both.

**Sense A — the curriculum as designed and enacted.** Along five dimensions (Context, Content, Pedagogy, Didactics, Assessment), each learning point is coded so that two curricula, or two versions of the same curriculum, can be compared without weeks of ad-hoc reading. The dimensions are derived from existing theory, not invented: Schwab’s commonplaces [VERIFY], Klafki’s *Didaktik* analysis [VERIFY], Chevallard’s didactic transposition [VERIFY], Carroll’s time-on-task (1963 [VERIFY]), Sweller’s cognitive load (1988), Black and Wiliam’s formative assessment (1998), Biggs’s constructive alignment [VERIFY], Wiggins and McTighe’s backward design (2005), Stufflebeam’s CIPP (2000), and the EQuIP rubric (NextGenScience, 2023). EQuIP is the closest relative; our delta is that codes are *computable* at learning-point grain and therefore *diffable* across versions.

**Sense B — the assessment of the assessment.** Constructive alignment and UbD both start from evidence of learning. If the battery cannot be trusted, Sense A’s outcome claims collapse — which is exactly what happened in our field experience. Sense B is therefore not an appendix; it is the Assessment dimension applied to our own instrument. The measurement failure is the object of analysis, not a confession to bury.

We do not score a curriculum “good / bad.” We report a profile: what was taught, how, to whom, under which constraints, and whether the evidence collected could possibly support the claims one would want to make.

### Sense B — how we assess *our* assessment

The question is not “did the test show learning?” It is “could this test show the learning the curriculum claims to produce?” Operational checks, all computable from the same tables:

1. **Coverage.** Share of enacted learning points with a primary item; share of *core* points if mastery is later assigned. Our rebuilt count: 17/51 assessed (33%), 6 partial (12%). Non-assessment is legitimate for exposure-tier points; it is a failure for points we then interpret as “students did/didn’t learn X.”
2. **Grain and provenance.** 23 administered items, 4-option MC + explicit IDK; mixed AICOS / author-made. Contrast: AI-CI uses 8–22 items *per construct*, built from documented misconceptions via cognitive interviews (Zhang, Perry & Lee, 2025). A 23-item omnibus cannot diagnose 51 learning points.
3. **Within-concept coherence.** Two items mapped to the same learning point must move together. `robotparts_1` vs `robotparts_2` (same W1_03) moved in **opposite** directions in the experimental group. That is an item-quality flag, not a student-knowledge flag.
4. **Chance and distractors.** Wilson CIs vs 25% chance; items whose interval sits on chance at T7 (GA04 in experimental) cannot support a “taught but not learned” vs “never tested fairly” distinction without further item work. AICOS already flags GA04 as *difficult* (parameter 1.01) — we imported a hard adult item into a one-slide middle-school treatment.
5. **IDK as a diagnostic, not missingness.** Rate change across waves; per-child stability (trait vs noise); **conversion:** among sequences that leave IDK, do they land correct or wrong? In the combined file, IDK→wrong exceeds IDK→correct (174 vs 136). Highest wrong-exit counts: `microcontrollers_2`, `robotparts_2`, **GA04** (12 wrong vs 2 correct; p(wrong\|exit) ≈ .86), `robotparts_1`, DA09/GA17/UA19. That pattern is how we see *plausible-distractor capture* and *teaching that does not convert uncertainty*.
6. **Scoring integrity.** `LLMdesign_1` was stored as option numbers 1–5, not 0/1/99. Codebook stem: “When designing a prompt for an LLM, which instruction could produce a worse response?” Correct = **c** “Tell me about science.” We recode 3→correct, {1,2,4}→wrong, and treat 5 as IDK (modal value, rises T1→T7) pending codebook confirmation. Raw-option analysis shows the misreading signature: at every wave, 27–36% of respondents pick one of the two *good* prompts (a or d) as the “worse” one — consistent with reading the negative stem as positive (“which instruction is best?”). That is attention to wording, not prompt-design knowledge (see check 9). `GA17` was administered (n≈50–58 per wave) with **no recoverable stem**. An assessment that cannot name its own items fails Sense B by construction.
7. **Subjective–objective dissociation.** MAILS × knowledge near zero is the *expected* finding (MAILS-SV r = .21; AICOS ≈ .04), not a failed validation. Self-report is a different construct; we keep both and do not average them.
8. **Affective alignment.** Weekly IMI joined to that week’s concept count and ICAP mix. We treat engagement as its own outcome (Four Pillars / IMI), never as proof of learning. First-pass week-level signals (n=7, exploratory): more new learning points ↔ lower enjoyment (ρ ≈ −.70); higher passive share ↔ lower enjoyment (ρ ≈ −.80). Hypothesis for teachers (inverted-U with workload) still needs the teacher-pulse file.
9. **Wording and cognitive-level audit.** Four of 23 stems are negatively worded: `sensors_2` (“can a sensor NOT perform”), `CA12` (“NOT AI”), `LLMdesign_1` (“a worse response”), `GA04` (“does NOT cause hallucinations”). Item-writing guidelines say avoid negative stems, or capitalize and boldface the negation (Haladyna, Downing, & Rodriguez, 2002, guideline 17); children and weaker readers systematically misrespond to negative items — a cognitive-developmental effect correlated with reading skill (Marsh, 1986; Borgers, Hox, & Sikkel, 2000 ⏳); and reverse-worded Likert items add inattention rather than control bias (van Sonderen, Sanderman, & Coyne, 2013 — relevant to our affect battery’s reversed attachment_4r/5r). Our German translation capitalizes NICHT in `sensors_2`/`CA12` but leaves GA04’s “keine” and LLMdesign_1’s “schlechteren/worse” unemphasized — and the two unemphasized negatives are among the weakest items (T7 .29 and .42; pooled negative stems .37→.31→.40 vs positive .42→.47→.51, n=4 items, illustrative). Bloom distribution is bottom-heavy: 4 Remember / 9 Understand / 5 Apply / 2 Analyze / 1 Evaluate (+1 stem missing); the single Evaluate item (GA16) *dropped* (−.10), so the battery is silent on higher-order outcomes. Extra flag: GA04’s stem *defines* hallucination as “nonsensical or grammatically incorrect” text — the surface-error misconception itself — contradicting the taught learning point W4_04 (plausible-but-false; always verify). A child who learned our lesson would be confused by the item’s premise; a child who did not has the misconception reinforced by the test.

v2 implication, already committed: **GA04 / “LLMs hallucinate; always verify” cannot remain a single slide.** It needs a dedicated activity (students catch a hallucination, then write a verification move) — Pedagogy (ICAP ≥ constructive, SAFE Active+Focused+Explicit), Didactics (misconception + high MAIL difficulty), and Assessment (replace or scaffold the imported AICOS item — rewritten with a positive stem and a stem definition matching the taught learning point; track IDK→wrong on that item as a success criterion).

The same nine checks are what a generation tool must expose for any draft it proposes: not only “here is a lesson,” but “here is how you would know it worked, and here is where the current evidence would be silent.”
