# Introduction

Children and adolescents are already massively interacting with artificial intelligence chatbots (Common Sense Media, 2025; Internet Matters, 2025; WIP-CH, 2025), using them in many different ways within a single conversation, often in the same apps they use to chat with friends, making it hard for parents to know what is happening (Common Sense Media, 2025). These interactions are embedded in a broader digital environment in which AI-generated content and algorithmic curation increasingly shape what children see and believe, tying AI literacy closely to digital and media literacy. The companies developing AI chatbots are often not transparent about whether or how they were designed to support children and adolescents’ learning, well-being and development. At the same time, children and adolescents are undergoing sensitive periods of development – periods during which environmental inputs are crucial (ref) –  and changing their environment can result in systemic impacts with lifelong consequences.

One response to protect children and adolescents has been AI literacy, aiming to teach the fundamentals of generative AI, how it impacts society and how to use it safely and appropriately. Several frameworks have been developed: UNESCO's AI Competency Framework for Students (UNESCO, 2024) and, building on it, the OECD/EU AI Literacy Framework (OECD & European Union, 2026), which now feeds into the PISA 2029 Media and AI Literacy assessment (OECD, 2026). All call for systematic AI education in schools.

The frameworks are intended to lay the foundations for curricula that can be used in classrooms, and many in the field responded with a wave of curricula and teaching materials — among them MIT's "The Brain Behind the Bot" (Day of AI, 2025), and offerings from Duke (Duke Libraries and CARADITE and Duke Center for Teaching and Learning, 2025), Harvard (metaLAB (at) Harvard, 2023), Georgia's AI4GA (Gelder et al., 2025), aiEDU (aiEDU, 2024), and Common Sense Media (Common Sense Education, 2023). This wave builds on earlier AI and digital literacy curricula developed before the ChatGPT revolution, such as AI4K12 (Touretzky et al., 2019), MIT's DAILy, code.org's AI activities (Code.org, 2023) and ENARIS (Graz University of Technology et al., 2022). The amount of materials, frameworks, tools and curricula is overwhelming, making it difficult to compare them and to keep track of the rapid evolution in the field. In summer 2025, many AI literacy activities were about prompt engineering (Khalid & Witmer, 2025) and verifying answers, but by summer 2026, the field had moved to context engineering (ref) and problems with sycophancy (Cheng et al., 2025), socio-affective pull (Neugnot-Cerioli, 2026), manipulation (Contro et al., 2025) and safeguarding (ref). To keep up with innovation, new knowledge and social transformation, curricula would need to be constantly, systematically and rapidly updated, validated, released and communicated upon. Moreover, it would require a substantial effort in teacher training and continuous professional development.

Designing a curriculum is recognised as a complex, lengthy, time and resource-intensive process (ref), and requires the integration of the purpose, target knowledge and competencies with the science of learning, developmental science, pedagogy, didactics and assessment (ref). A standard curriculum may need to be adapted to the context, such as integrating students' prior knowledge, available time and resources, and teacher expertise. Considering this many variables simultaneously stretches human cognitive capacity and, in practice, risks resulting in suboptimal teaching and learning. By contrast, state-of-the-art large language models (LLMs) can hold many such variables in view at once, creating an opportunity to analyse existing curricula, improve understanding of what works, and generate draft curricula adapted to specific contexts, with updated materials for teachers to review and adjust.

A curriculum's effectiveness is rarely evaluated with pre- and post-tests including long-term retention, and even more rarely with the goal of improving the curriculum itself. When an evaluation exists, improvement is mainly guided qualitatively, as a quantitative approach has until now been labor-intensive and costly. However, LLMs are particularly well positioned for executing such a task at speed and with quality: curricula are made of slide decks, PDF lesson plans, and activity worksheets, and with LLMs we can now ask questions such as: How many new concepts are introduced per hour? Which concepts are revisited, and after what interval? What fraction of learning objectives is ever assessed? How is time divided between exposition, practice, and discussion? It is now possible to create a standardized, systematic analysis of curricula, improving comparability –  notably longitudinally and beyond a black-box approach –  and making iteration, continuous improvement and updating possible.

In this paper, we propose an AI and digital literacy curriculum analysis and generation tool that builds on our experience of developing such a curriculum and implementing it in a German school, collecting data on learning, motivation and engagement. This rich field experience allows us to present lessons learned both from the process of developing a curriculum and from analyzing the results in relation to it. We start by describing how we developed our curriculum, which involved an analysis of 15 existing curricula. We describe the developed curriculum and present the results. A distinctive feature of our approach is that we started by listing concrete learning points – the exact knowledge we wanted students to retain –  rather than abstract competencies, thereby operationalizing competencies into a reportable and assessable unit. Then, we propose an operationalized curriculum analysis based on a five-dimension framework – context, content, pedagogy, didactics, and assessment – and present its application to our curriculum, linking it to learning, motivation and engagement outcomes, including an assessment of the assessment itself. Finally, we conclude with a proposal for an improved AI and digital literacy curriculum. 

This work is one case study and a proof of concept, from a single school context, with exploratory analyses and a generation tool that has not yet been classroom-validated. Our ambition is to show the potential transformation in the field to make curricula easier to describe, compare, and improve over time, and to open a conversation about the tools that are becoming possible and how they can improve the field.
