We used a combination of methods: personal recommendations (KI-Aufträge by University of Berlin, AI Adapt by Gillian Barber, Common Sense Media), a query on Elicit (What are existing open source and free AI literacy curriculums for primary, secondary and high schools? Which countries, language and age group they cover? what knowledge, competences, skills? How many cover Ethical AI and risks & benefits of AI?) (of the 10 curriculums identified, only MIT curriculums matched our age group and open-source free requirements), and targeted internet search (ENARIS by EPFL, Toddle 101 Course, code.org, Duke university, Georgia's AI4GA, AI pedagogy by Harvard, AI4k12). The search was done in English and probably missed resources in other languages. The goal was not to be exhaustive, but to find major curriculums in AI literacy to guide the teaching in the project.

For the detailed curriculum (i.e., slides and activities) we encountered several difficulties that led us to create our own curriculum, recombining and adapting some existing elements with new ones: in fact, the reuse of an existing curriculum was complicated by the intersection of the need to have up-to-date materials to include LLMs, be adapted for 13/14 year old students, covering our learning objectives and fitting in our scheduled time, all while maintaining coherence, pedagogical soundness and flow. Duke and Harvard curriculums were excluded because considered too advanced, AI adapt, AI4k12 and ENARIS by EPFL were developed before large public use of LLMs and didn't cover some of our learning goals. Georgia's curriculum was too technical and long while common sense media too introductory. aiEDU was focused on critical thinking about application and use of AI in education, but not on how LLMs work. Berlin's curriculum, while proposing several interesting activities, also included python, machine learning and data analysis exercises and project based learning, while missing explicit explanation of how LLMs work, ethics and sustainability. MIT curriculums represented the best match and we reused several activities from those curriculums. MIT's "The brain Behind the Bot" had the best explanation of what is an LLM and how it works, but it lacked the connection to the larger

concept of AI. We therefore combined elements from DAILY MIT curriculum about what is AI, difference between classifying and generating, the different machine learning types and the 3 steps of the development of an LLM described in "The brain Behind the Bot". To make the link, we reused the names of the learning types at the 3 stages: unsupervised, supervised and reinforcement learning. We also included elements from code.org on biases and Toddle 101 Course on the uses of AI which matched our desired level of detail and timing.

## Discussion

### Curriculum

- Very difficult to do a controlled curriculum in the wild

- Too little time, too many topics

- Things take a lot longer

- Technical failures

- A lot of work, ideally should be iterative, collaborative process where others build on our work (e.g., Buildbots)

- Teachers did not have enough training to effectively present the material to students -> more need for teacher training

### Measurements

- We had to develop measures to test the success of our curriculum because a lot of other curriculums are not providing measurements

- better AI literacy measures needed

- The measurements that exist are not tailored to specific curriculum / geographical contexts / age groups / skill levels of students

- Measurements were too complex, and do not assess all types of learning -> class discussions would be beneficial for that, we could also do assessments with AI (LLM paper citation)
