## 2.2 Analysis of existing curricula

Before developing our own materials, we reviewed existing AI-literacy curricula through three pragmatic channels: (i) personal recommendations from AI-literacy experts (Berlin's *KI-Aufträge*, AI Adapt, Common Sense Media); (ii) a targeted query on the Elicit research platform asking for open-source, free AI-literacy curricula, their countries, languages, age groups, and content coverage — of the ten curricula returned, only MIT's matched our age and open-access requirements; and (iii) targeted web searches for major curricula (ENARIS by EPFL, Toddle AI 101, code.org, Duke Learning Innovation, Georgia's AI4GA, Harvard's AI Pedagogy Project, AI4K12). We retained curricula that (a) target ages 12–16, (b) are freely available, (c) exist in English or German, and (d) cover AI or LLM content rather than general digital literacy. Fifteen curricula met these criteria (Table 1). The search was conducted in English and German and is not exhaustive: our goal was not a systematic review but a representative map of what a teacher team could realistically find and reuse.

The corpus is heterogeneous on every dimension we recorded (Table 1): duration ranges from two-hour workshops (AI in My Life) to full-semester courses (Georgia AI4GA); formats span slide decks, mission-based platforms, web modules, and resource directories; technical depth ranges from introductory awareness (Common Sense Media) to university-level treatment (Duke, Harvard). To compare content beyond publishers' descriptions, we extracted the specific learning points each curriculum teaches — concrete, checkable statements of knowledge rather than competency labels — and rated depth of coverage per point (0 = not covered to 3 = deep coverage with activities; 200+ learning points across 13 curricula; Supplement [S-X]). This granularity revealed a fragmented division of labour. ENARIS provides the deepest technical coverage (~65 points at depth 3, including the only detailed treatments of reinforcement learning, face-detection algorithms, and privacy regulation), Georgia AI4GA the most hands-on activities, MIT DAILy the strongest ethics strand, Berlin the only advanced prompt-engineering content, and MIT's *The Brain Behind the Bot* the clearest explanation of LLM mechanics. Conversely, 40 learning points were covered by fewer than three curricula at depth ≥ 2, and topics we consider urgent for current generative-AI users were nearly absent: in our extraction, LLM hallucination was treated in depth by only one curriculum (Day of AI) and mentioned or explored by two more (Duke; Georgia), while the deliberate human-likeness of chatbot design — a safeguard-relevant topic — appeared only at the margins (e.g., aiEDU's "avoid anthropomorphizing" competency; Common Sense Media's lesson on why chatbots sound human). No reviewed curriculum addressed the prediction-as-oracle misconception (the belief that an AI prediction about an individual is a fixed fate rather than a population-level tendency).

No single curriculum satisfied the intersection of our constraints: up-to-date LLM coverage, age-fit for 13–14-year-olds, alignment with our learning objectives, seven weeks of 140 minutes, German delivery, available hardware, and pedagogical coherence as a sequence. Each candidate failed differently. Duke and Harvard were too advanced; AI Adapt, AI4K12, and ENARIS predated public generative AI and missed several of our learning objectives; Georgia was too long and too technical; Common Sense Media too introductory; aiEDU addressed critical use of AI applications but not how LLMs work; Berlin offered strong activities but no explicit explanation of LLMs, and no ethics or sustainability coverage. MIT's materials came closest. We therefore adopted a **recombination strategy**: from MIT DAILy we took AI basics, the classification-versus-generation distinction, and the machine-learning types; from *The Brain Behind the Bot* the three-stage LLM training pipeline — reusing the learning-type names (unsupervised, supervised, reinforcement) to bind the two sources into one narrative; from code.org, bias activities; from Toddle, uses of AI at an appropriate level of detail. Content that existed nowhere became our original contributions: social robots as embodied AI, LLM deception and safeguards, AI sustainability, and the connection between physical robots and LLMs (§2.3).

Three observations from this process generalize beyond our case. **First, there is no shared descriptive language.** Curricula organize content as missions, modules, lessons, or "big ideas," and none states its taught knowledge at a granularity that permits direct comparison; our learning-point extraction required weeks of expert reading for 13 curricula. The analysis cost alone is a reuse barrier — and even our own two analysis passes did not fully agree (see evidence map), which underscores how difficult these artifacts are to analyse consistently. **Second, the temporal lag is structural.** Curricula built before 2023 cannot cover generative AI, and curriculum development cycles are slower than the technology they target (§1) — reuse of an older curriculum means inheriting its obsolescence. **Third, assessment support is nearly absent.** A systematic pass over all fifteen curricula for embedded assessments with solutions found exactly one — ENARIS — providing ready-to-use quizzes with answers and explanations embedded in the slide decks, plus separate solution files for programming exercises, requiring no preparation beyond display. The remaining curricula offer discussion prompts without answer keys (Common Sense Media, Duke), external quiz tools (AI Adapt), or assignments without formal solutions (Berlin's Python/Pandas tasks, MIT's worksheets). No curriculum includes automated grading or coded evaluation tools, and **no reviewed curriculum maps its concepts to its assessments at all**. Teachers thus inherit not only adaptation work — content, pacing, standards alignment — but also the time-intensive task of developing evaluation instruments and answer keys from scratch, a barrier that disproportionately affects practitioners without curriculum-design training.

Finally, we note what the literature does not contain. In a corpus of 85 sources spanning AI-literacy frameworks, curricula, assessments, and adjacent fields, we found no empirical analysis of how AI-literacy curricula are actually developed — who builds them, how long it takes, what gets reused, and why reuse fails [to our knowledge; to be hardened with a systematic Scopus/WoS query before submission]. Curriculum development practice remains a black box, which may be why each team — including ours — ends up re-solving the same problems. The field experience reported in §2.3–2.5 is our first response; the reporting standard proposed in §3 is designed to make the next team's analysis cheaper than ours.
