# AI Literacy Assessments: Instruments, Frameworks, and Measurement Challenges

> Summary of the scientific literature on how AI literacy is — and should be — measured. Covers existing validated instruments, major measurement frameworks, the self-report vs. performance gap, and implications for curriculum evaluation.

---

## 1. What Is Being Measured? Definitions and Dimensions

AI literacy is widely understood as an individual's confidence and capability to explain how AI technologies function and affect society, and to use those technologies ethically and responsibly (Yang et al., 2025, citing multiple frameworks). Key dimensions recur across frameworks:


| Dimension                  | Description                                                                  |
| -------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Conceptual / technical** | Understanding how AI systems work (e.g., machine learning, data, algorithms) |
| **Critical appraisal**     | Evaluating AI outputs, detecting bias, assessing reliability                 |
| **Practical / procedural** | Using AI tools effectively in real tasks                                     |
| **Ethical**                | Understanding societal impacts, fairness, privacy, accountability            |
| **Affective**              | Attitudes, self-efficacy, motivation toward AI                               |


A key distinction, with direct implications for assessment, is between **declarative knowledge** (knowing *about* AI) and **procedural competence** (knowing *how to act with* AI). Clerc et al. (2026) demonstrated empirically that these are not the same: self-reported AI knowledge did not predict task performance in middle-school students, whereas behavioral regulation during AI-assisted tasks did.

## 2. Major Assessment Frameworks

### 2.1 PISA 2029 Media and AI Literacy (MAIL) Framework (OECD, 2026)

The OECD's first international large-scale assessment of AI and media literacy will be included in PISA 2029. The MAIL framework defines AI literacy as "the technical knowledge, durable skills and future-ready attitudes required to thrive in a world influenced by AI systems" (OECD, 2026, p. 9). It integrates media literacy and AI literacy as interdependent, given that AI increasingly generates, curates, and mediates media content.

**Five competence areas with developmental progressions:**


| Competence                                | Focus                                                  |
| ----------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| Reflect and Act Ethically and Responsibly | Ethical use; rights, privacy, fairness                 |
| Access and Use                            | Finding and using AI/media tools purposefully          |
| Analyse and Evaluate                      | Critically appraising AI-generated content and outputs |
| Participate and Collaborate               | Civic engagement; collaborative knowledge creation     |
| Create                                    | Producing media and AI-assisted content responsibly    |


Each competence includes a draft student expectation progression table (Tables 4.1–4.5 in the document), making it one of the few frameworks that explicitly defines what assessment should look like at different levels. Assessment tasks are designed around realistic contexts (chat tools, browsers, news articles) and include both selected-response and constructed-response formats.

**Why it matters for curriculum evaluation:** PISA MAIL will provide the first internationally comparable data on AI and media literacy across countries, enabling benchmarking and identifying gaps in national curricula.

> **Reference:** OECD. (2026). *First draft of the PISA 2029 Media and Artificial Intelligence Literacy (MAIL) Assessment Framework*. OECD.

---

### 2.2 UNESCO AI Competency Framework — Assessment Component (UNESCO, 2024)

The UNESCO AI Competency Framework for Students (see also curriculum evaluation document) includes competency-based assessment guidance (Table 5) across three progression levels (Understand, Apply, Create) and four dimensions (human-centred mindset, ethics, techniques and applications, system design). Assessment tasks are mapped to each competency block and progression level, with a focus on performance evidence rather than recall.

> **Reference:** UNESCO. (2024). *AI competency framework for students*. UNESCO. [https://doi.org/10.54675/JKJB9835](https://doi.org/10.54675/JKJB9835)

---

### 2.3 Long & Magerko (2020) Competency Map

Long and Magerko's (2020) foundational literature review identified 17 competencies for AI literacy, organised around the ability to *critically evaluate AI*, *understand AI*, and *use AI*, plus five design considerations for learner-centred AI tools. This competency map has been widely adopted as an implicit assessment framework — many instruments are designed to test subsets of these competencies.

> **Reference:** Long, D., & Magerko, B. (2020). What is AI literacy? Competencies and design considerations. *Proceedings of the CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems*. [https://doi.org/10.1145/3313831.3376727](https://doi.org/10.1145/3313831.3376727)

## 3. Validated Instruments

### 3.1 AI Literacy Concept Inventory (AI-CI) — Middle School (Zhang, Perry & Lee, 2025)

**Type:** Performance-based (objective), multiple-choice  
**Items:** 20  
**Population:** Middle school (grades 5–8)  
**Validated with:** 981 students; pre/post reliability with 108 students via DAILy curriculum

Developed at MIT, the AI-CI assesses conceptual understanding across four topics: AI general concepts, logic systems, machine learning general concepts, and supervised learning. Validation included expert panel review, classroom observations, and cognitive interviews. It is among the few instruments validated specifically for pre-adolescent learners and anchored to a defined curriculum (DAILy, ~32 hours).

**Limitation:** Covers technical/conceptual dimensions only; does not assess ethical reasoning or procedural competence.

> **Reference:** Zhang, H., Perry, A., & Lee, I. (2025). Developing and validating the Artificial Intelligence Literacy Concept Inventory: An instrument to assess AI literacy among middle school students. *International Journal of Artificial Intelligence in Education*. [https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1461172](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1461172)

---

### 3.2 AI Literacy Questionnaire (AILQ) — Secondary (Ng et al., 2024)

**Type:** Self-report questionnaire  
**Items:** 32  
**Population:** Secondary school students  
**Validated with:** 363 students (Hong Kong)

The AILQ uses an ABCE (Affective–Behavioural–Cognitive–Ethical) framework:

- **Affective:** motivation, self-efficacy
- **Behavioural:** commitment, collaboration
- **Cognitive:** know/understand; apply, evaluate and create
- **Ethical:** ethical reasoning about AI use

Validated through confirmatory factor analysis with strong psychometric properties across all four dimensions. Provides broader coverage than performance-based tests, but is subject to self-report bias (see Section 4).

> **Reference:** Ng, D. T. K., Leung, J. K. L., Chu, S. K. W., & Lee, A. V. Y. (2024). Design and validation of the AI literacy questionnaire: The affective, behavioural, cognitive and ethical approach. *British Journal of Educational Technology, 55*(3). [https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13411](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13411)

---

### 3.3 MAILS — Meta AI Literacy Scale (Carolus et al., 2023)

**Type:** Self-report questionnaire  
**Items:** 34 (short form: 10 items, Koch et al., 2024)  
**Population:** General population / adults; used with adolescents in some studies

MAILS is grounded in competency models and psychological meta-competencies. It measures AI literacy as a multidimensional construct, with a short validated 10-item version available for research contexts where brevity matters. The scale has been used in longitudinal studies of attitudes toward AI and in the BuildBots AI literacy study (as MAILS; referenced in CurriculumPaper.md).

**Limitation:** Not specifically validated for middle school (pre-teen) populations.

> **References:**  
> Carolus, A., Koch, M. J., Straka, S., Lauk, M., & Wienrich, C. (2023). MAILS — Meta AI literacy scale: Development and testing of an AI literacy questionnaire based on well-founded competency models and psychological change and meta-competencies. *Computers in Human Behavior: Artificial Humans, 1*(1), 100014.  
> Koch, M. J., et al. (2024). Validating and refining a short version of the MAILS. *(publication details require verification)*

### 3.4 Rasch-Validated Multidimensional AI Literacy Scale (2025)

**Type:** Self-report, Rasch-calibrated  
**Dimensions:** Technological understanding, critical appraisal, practical application, AI ethics

A 2025 study used Rasch Model analysis to validate a multidimensional AI literacy scale for education, removing items with poor fit to improve measurement precision (Humanities and Social Sciences Communications, 2025). Rasch modeling provides interval-level measurement and fit statistics that go beyond standard confirmatory factor analysis, making this one of the more psychometrically rigorous instruments available.

> **Reference:** [Author names require verification]. (2025). Validating and refining a multi-dimensional scale for measuring AI literacy in education using the Rasch Model. *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications*. [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41599-025-05670-6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41599-025-05670-6)

### 3.5 Generative AI Literacy Assessment Test (GLAT) — Higher Education (Stanford SCALE, 2024)

**Type:** Performance-based (objective), multiple-choice  
**Items:** 20  
**Population:** Higher education  
**Reliability:** Cronbach's α = 0.80; omega = 0.81

GLAT uses both classical test theory and item response theory. External validity was established by showing that GLAT scores predict actual performance on GenAI-supported tasks — a crucial property given the self-report/performance gap problem. Though designed for higher education, it provides a methodological model for developing similar instruments for K–12.

> **Reference:** Stanford SCALE Initiative. (2024). *GLAT: The Generative AI Literacy Assessment Test*. [https://scale.stanford.edu/ai/repository/glat-generative-ai-literacy-assessment-test](https://scale.stanford.edu/ai/repository/glat-generative-ai-literacy-assessment-test); [https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00283](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00283)

## 4. The Self-Report vs. Performance Gap

This is a critical methodological problem for AI literacy assessment, with direct empirical evidence.

### 4.1 The Gap Is Real and Large

A 2024 systematic review of AI literacy scales (Lintner, 2024) found that out of 16 validated scales, only 3 were performance-based — the rest were self-report. Yet self-report and performance measures diverge substantially:

- Bewersdorff et al. (preprint) analysed 31 empirical studies using six AI literacy instruments across 14 countries and found that "self-assessment scales show systematically higher correlations compared with a performance-based test, hinting at metacognitive bias in self-reports"
- A 2026 study found low correlation between self-report and objective-based factors, identifying distinct profiles including overestimation (self-report > performance), underestimation, and alignment (Stanford SCALE, 2026)
- Clerc et al. (2026) found that neither overall GenAI self-report scores nor metacognitive self-report scores predicted task performance in middle-school students; behavioral indicators of regulation did

### 4.2 Overconfidence Is Stronger in Younger Learners

Miscalibration between self-reported competence and actual performance is stronger in younger individuals for digital skills (Palczyńska & Rynko, 2021, as cited in Clerc et al., 2026). Abdelghani et al. (2025) found that middle-school students' self-reported AI expertise correlated *negatively* with actual performance — i.e., the more confident students were, the worse they performed.

### 4.3 Implication for Curriculum Evaluation

Self-report measures of AI literacy (attitudes, confidence, perceived competence) are insufficient on their own to evaluate whether a curriculum has worked. Performance-based measures, behavioral observation, or task-embedded assessment are needed to accurately capture learning gains.

## 5. What Existing Instruments Lack

The systematic review of AI literacy scales (Lintner, 2024) assessed all 16 identified scales using the COSMIN quality framework. Key gaps:


| Quality property                       | Status                           |
| -------------------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| Structural validity                    | Generally adequate across scales |
| Internal consistency                   | Generally adequate               |
| Content validity                       | Only a few scales tested         |
| Construct validity                     | Only a few scales tested         |
| Cross-cultural validity                | **None** of the scales tested    |
| Measurement error                      | **None** of the scales tested    |
| Responsiveness (sensitivity to change) | Rarely reported                  |


Additional gaps identified across the literature (Zhang et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025; Ng et al., 2023):

- No single standardised instrument is validated across educational levels, cultures, and age groups
- Most instruments are not sensitive to the specific competencies being taught in a given curriculum — they measure general AI literacy rather than curriculum-specific outcomes
- Ethics and human–AI relationship components are rarely assessed, even in curricula that claim to teach them
- Assessment of GenAI-specific literacy (prompting, evaluating outputs, detecting hallucinations) is poorly covered by instruments developed before 2022

## 6. Assessment Approaches by Method


| Method                               | Examples                                                             | Strengths                                                | Weaknesses                                                                    |
| ------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Performance-based (objective)**    | AI-CI, GLAT, task scores in Clerc et al. (2026)                      | Directly measures competence; avoids overestimation bias | Time-intensive; requires curriculum-specific design                           |
| **Self-report questionnaire**        | MAILS, AILQ, GenAI questionnaires                                    | Quick; scalable; captures attitudes                      | Systematically overestimates competence; poor predictor of actual performance |
| **Behavioral / process observation** | Prompt acceptance/rejection, follow-up querying (Clerc et al., 2026) | Ecologically valid; captures regulation in action        | Requires structured task design; hard to scale                                |
| **Artifact/project assessment**      | Final projects in Williams et al. (2022)                             | Captures application and creation                        | Subjective without rubrics; not standardized                                  |
| **Teacher observation / rating**     | Lesson-level feedback in CurriculumPaper context                     | Captures classroom dynamics and difficulty               | High subjectivity; not comparable across settings                             |
| **Large-scale standardised**         | PISA 2029 MAIL                                                       | Internationally comparable; externally validated         | Not yet deployed; results only from 2030 onward                               |

## 7. Age-Appropriateness of Current Instruments


| Instrument                   | Age range                   | Performance-based?      | Ethical dimension? |
| ---------------------------- | --------------------------- | ----------------------- | ------------------ |
| AI-CI (Zhang et al., 2025)   | Grades 5–8 (~10–14)         | Yes                     | No                 |
| AILQ (Ng et al., 2024)       | Secondary (~13–18)          | No (self-report)        | Yes                |
| MAILS (Carolus et al., 2023) | Adults / general population | No (self-report)        | Partially          |
| GLAT (Stanford, 2024)        | Higher education            | Yes                     | Partially          |
| PISA MAIL (OECD, 2026)       | 15-year-olds                | Yes (performance tasks) | Yes                |


**Gap:** No validated, performance-based AI literacy instrument currently exists for primary school children (ages 6–11). For middle school (ages 11–14), the AI-CI is the best available option but covers only technical understanding.

---

## 8. Implications for the BuildBots / CurriculumPaper Context

Based on the literature, the following observations apply to AI literacy measurement in the BuildBots study context:

1. **MAILS** was used as a self-report measure at T1, T4, T7 — consistent with current practice but subject to overestimation bias and poor sensitivity to specific learning gains
2. **Objective learning items** developed for the study provide the performance-based complement needed to overcome MAILS limitations — this is methodologically sound
3. The CurriculumPaper draft notes that "measurements were too complex and do not assess all types of learning" — consistent with Lintner (2024) findings that no instrument covers all AI literacy dimensions
4. Class discussions and qualitative feedback (mentioned in the draft) are appropriate for assessing ethical reasoning and human–AI relationship competencies, which no current validated scale covers adequately
5. The absence of cross-culturally validated instruments means Swiss-context adaptations, as done in the study, are necessary and defensible
