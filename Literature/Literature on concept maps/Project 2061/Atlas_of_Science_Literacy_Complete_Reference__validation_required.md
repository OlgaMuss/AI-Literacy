# PART II: MAP KEY - How to Read the Strand Maps

## Benchmark Codes Explained

A benchmark code like **1B/M1b*** contains:

| Component | Meaning | Example |
|-----------|---------|---------|
| First number | Chapter in Benchmarks | 1 = Nature of Science |
| Letter after number | Section in chapter | B = Scientific Inquiry |
| Letter before slash | Grade range | P=K-2, E=3-5, M=6-8, H=9-12 |
| Number after slash | Benchmark number in grade range | 1 = first benchmark |
| Lowercase letter | Sentence within benchmark | b = second sentence |
| Asterisk (*) | Status: edited from original | * = edited |
| Double asterisk (**) | Status: new benchmark | ** = new |

### Source Abbreviations for New Benchmarks:
- SFAA = Science for All Americans
- NSES = National Science Education Standards
- BSL = Benchmarks for Science Literacy
- ASL = Atlas of Science Literacy, Volume 1

## Visual Elements on Maps

| Element | Meaning |
|---------|---------|
| **Colored boxes** | Knowledge benchmarks |
| **Bordered boxes** | Skill benchmarks |
| **Solid arrows** | Connection within map (achieving one contributes to the other) |
| **Dashed arrows** | Connection to benchmarks on other maps |
| **Grade bands (rows)** | K-2, 3-5, 6-8, 9-12 |
| **Strands (columns)** | Loosely related groups of benchmarks that develop over time |

# PART III: METHODOLOGY SUMMARY (from Vol 1 Reflections)

## Origin of Strand Maps

The strand maps grew out of Project 2061's conceptual perspective on K-12 education. The mapping approach is tied to the vision of science literacy articulated in *Science for All Americans* and how science education needs to change.

### Key Development Principles:

1. **Growth of Understanding Focus**: Maps portray literacy from the viewpoint of how students are likely to develop knowledge, providing perspective on content helpful for educators thinking about how to teach it.

2. **Connections Among Benchmarks**: The essential task is to think through the entire flow of learning, showing the ideas and skills students should achieve over time rather than the instruction they would receive.

3. **Multiple Entry Points**: Mapmakers found it useful to approach maps from different directions - some started from adult literacy goals and worked backward, others began with first-step benchmarks and built toward literacy.

## Map Construction Process

### Step 1: Clarifying Benchmarks
- Making a map begins with studying a single benchmark
- Explore precisely what ideas students are expected to know and what goes beyond the benchmark
- Topic-level descriptions are not adequate; precise understanding of concepts is essential

### Step 2: Identifying Related Benchmarks
Two guiding questions drive the search:
- What should students know before approaching this benchmark?
- What ideas might this benchmark contribute to later?

Finding relevant benchmarks involves exploring strands—groups of increasingly sophisticated benchmarks that run through sections in Benchmarks.

### Step 3: Making Connections
- Connections between benchmarks are explicit in maps but were only implicit in Benchmarks
- Making maps entails hunting down implied connections and sometimes clarifying or supplementing benchmarks
- Arrows indicate that achieving one benchmark contributes to achieving another

## Types of Connections

Arrows represent various relationships:
- A benchmark may be a **necessary prerequisite** to a later benchmark
- A benchmark may **support or enhance** understanding of a later benchmark
- A benchmark may provide **evidence** that supports an idea in a later benchmark
- A benchmark may **reinforce** an earlier benchmark through specific examples

## Strands and Neighborhoods

- **Strands**: Labeled groups at the bottom of maps that loosely suggest groups of benchmarks developing over multiple grade ranges
- **Neighborhoods**: Alternative groupings of related benchmarks (considered but not used in final maps)
- Strands were chosen because they suggested more strongly separable topics and were less intrusive on map design

## Map Size Considerations

- Connections among benchmarks are prolific enough that all could conceivably be put together into one vast map
- Design constraints, readability, and usefulness demanded reasonable numbers of related benchmarks per map
- Maps are not all the same size; decisions were made according to content coherence

# PART IV: USING THE MAPS

## For Curriculum Design

The maps help:
- Identify prerequisite knowledge for any benchmark
- See what ideas a benchmark contributes to later
- Understand the full context of any learning goal
- Recognize connections across different subject areas

## For Instruction

Maps help teachers:
- Point out areas of concern in curriculum
- Discuss what needs to happen in particular courses and grades
- Step back and think about the bigger picture
- Find interesting perspectives less apparent in lists of benchmarks

## For Assessment

In textbook evaluation, strand maps help:
- Recognize whether proposed instruction addresses the specific benchmark or only the general topic heading
- Determine whether content is presented at an appropriate level of sophistication
- Point out precursors and important connections textbook developers should consider
