## Core Theoretical Foundations (Verified Sources)

### 1. John Dewey - "Learning by Doing"

**Full Citation:**
Dewey, J. (1938). *Experience and education*. New York: Macmillan.

**Verified Access:**
- Full text available: https://archive.org/details/experienceeducat00dewe
- PDF download: https://archive.org/download/experienceeducat00dewe_0/experienceeducat00dewe_0.pdf
- Alternative scan: https://archive.org/details/experienceeducat00john

**Key Concepts:**
- **"Learning by doing"**: True education arises from experience
- Experience must be coupled with **reflection** to become meaningful learning
- Schools should function as laboratories where students solve real-world problems
- Emphasizes **active engagement** vs. passive absorption of information
- Education through **continuity and interaction** with the world

**Relevance to BuildBots:**
- Both assembly and robot operation involve "doing" and "experience"
- Active problem-solving (whether assembling parts or controlling robot movements)

### 2. David Kolb - Experiential Learning Theory

**Full Citation:**
Kolb, D. A. (1984). *Experiential learning: Experience as the source of learning and development*. Englewood Cliffs, NJ: Prentice-Hall.
- ISBN-10: 0132952610
- ISBN-13: 978-0132952613

**Second Edition:**
Kolb, D. A. (2015). *Experiential learning: Experience as the source of learning and development* (2nd ed.). Pearson Education.

**Verified Access:**
- Full text PDF: https://nsee.memberclicks.net/assets/docs/KnowledgeCenter/BuildingExpEduc/BooksReports/27.%20experiential%20learning%20learning%20as%20the%20source.pdf
- Alternative: https://carleton-wp-production.s3.amazonaws.com/uploads/sites/313/2022/12/Experiential_Learning_Experience_As_The_Source_Of_-1.pdf
- Citation info: https://www.scirp.org/reference/referencespapers?referenceid=1223948

**Key Framework: Four-Stage Experiential Learning Cycle**

1. **Concrete Experience (CE)**: "Hands-on" tangible experience (the "doing" phase)
2. **Reflective Observation (RO)**: Reviewing and reflecting on the experience  
3. **Abstract Conceptualization (AC)**: Forming mental models and theories
4. **Active Experimentation (AE)**: Testing concepts in real/simulated environment, leading to new experiences

**Core Definition:**
> "Knowledge is created through the transformation of experience"

**Relevance to BuildBots:**
- Stage 1 (Concrete Experience) = **both** assembly AND robot operation qualify
- Both involve tangible, hands-on experience followed by reflection
- Assembling: physical manipulation of components
- Operating: physical manipulation of controls/interfaces

### 3. Jerome Bruner - Modes of Representation

**Full Citation:**
Bruner, J. S. (1966). *Toward a theory of instruction*. Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press.

**Verified Access:**
- Citation: https://www.scirp.org/reference/referencespapers?referenceid=1457921
- Scribd (requires account): https://www.scribd.com/document/704914364/Bruner-J-S-1966-Toward-a-Theory-of-Instruction-Vol-59-Harvard-University-Press
- Overview: https://www.simplypsychology.org/bruner.html

**Three Modes of Representation:**

1. **Enactive Representation (Action-based)** ← **THIS IS HANDS-ON**
   - Knowledge stored as **motor responses** and **physical actions**
   - Primary for infants but remains vital for adults (e.g., muscle memory)
   - **Learning approach**: "Learning by doing" through direct, hands-on interaction

2. **Iconic Representation (Image-based)**
   - Information encoded as sensory images, mental pictures, diagrams
   - Uses visual aids to bridge concrete and abstract

3. **Symbolic Representation (Language-based)**
   - Knowledge stored through abstract systems (language, math symbols)
   - Enables manipulation of ideas without direct physical reference

**Key Pedagogical Concepts:**
- **Spiral Curriculum**: Complex subjects can be taught at any age if structured appropriately
- **Scaffolding**: Temporary support to help learner master tasks beyond current capability
- **Discovery Learning**: Active exploration where students discover principles themselves

**Critical Point:**
Bruner viewed modes as **integrated and loosely sequential** - they coexist and translate into one another throughout life. Unlike Piaget's rigid stages, learners use all modes.

**Relevance to BuildBots:**
- **Assembly**: Enactive mode (motor actions to fit parts together)
- **Operation**: Enactive mode (motor actions to control robot)
- Both involve physical actions and motor responses = hands-on
- Hands-on is not just a "style" but a **foundational stage of cognitive development**

### 4. VARK Model - Kinesthetic Learning

**Original Citation (CORRECTED):**
Fleming, N. D., & Mills, C. (1992). Not another inventory, rather a catalyst for reflection. *To Improve the Academy*, 11, 137–155.

**Note:** The VARK model was introduced in 1992, not 2001 as I initially stated.

**Later Publication:**
Fleming, N. D. (2001). *Teaching and learning styles: VARK strategies*. Christchurch, New Zealand: Neil Fleming.

**Verified Access:**
- Official VARK website: https://vark-learn.com/introduction-to-vark/
- VARK modalities: https://vark-learn.com/introduction-to-vark/the-vark-modalities/
- Original article: https://www.vark-learn.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/08/Educational-Developments.pdf
- PubMed overview: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24823519/

**Four Modalities:**
- **V**isual: Learning through seeing (diagrams, charts, maps)
- **A**ural: Learning through hearing (lectures, discussions)
- **R**ead/write: Learning through written words
- **K**inesthetic: **Learning through doing, physical movement, touch, sensory experience**

**Kinesthetic Learning Characteristics:**
- Prefer building, crafting, creating
- Struggle with traditional lecture-based or text-heavy environments
- Need to move (pacing, tapping) to maintain focus
- Thrive with: lab experiments, role-playing, simulations, **interactive activities**

**Instructional Strategies for Kinesthetic Learners:**
- Active engagement: experiments, field trips, simulations
- **Manipulatives**: Physical models, building kits
- Movement-based learning: physical activity during study
- **Using tools and equipment**

**Important Caveat:**
Modern educational psychology considers "learning styles" a **neuromyth** - research shows no evidence that matching instruction to "style" improves outcomes. However, the **definition of kinesthetic/hands-on activities remains valid** as a pedagogical approach that benefits ALL learners.

**Relevance to BuildBots:**
- Kinesthetic = learning by **doing**
- Both assembly and operation involve physical activity and motor engagement
- "Using tools and equipment" explicitly mentioned as kinesthetic

### 5. Maria Montessori - Concrete to Abstract

**Key Work:**
Montessori, M. (1917). *The advanced Montessori method*. New York: Frederick A. Stokes Company.

**Core Principle:**
Learning progresses: **Concrete → Representational → Abstract**

**Hands-On Materials (Manipulatives):**
- **Golden Beads**: Experience place value and arithmetic operations
- **Geometric Solids**: Explore shapes, volume, spatial relationships
- **Number Rods, Spindle Boxes**: Sensory-based number sense
- **Practical Life Materials**: Tools for pouring, fastening, using utensils

**Definition of Hands-On:**
- Using **tangible materials** to understand abstract concepts
- Physical manipulation precedes symbolic understanding
- Materials often involve **operating/using** tools (not just building them)

**Key Insight:**
Montessori materials include both:
- Materials children **assemble** (e.g., geometric puzzles)
- Materials children **use/operate** (e.g., pouring water, using tongs)

**Both are considered hands-on/manipulative learning**

**Relevance to BuildBots:**
- Supports idea that both construction AND use/operation are hands-on
- Physical engagement with tools/materials = hands-on, regardless of whether building or operating

## Synthesis: What Counts as "Hands-On Learning"?

### Consensus Definition Across Theorists:

**Hands-on learning** involves:

1. ✓ **Active physical engagement** (Dewey, 1938; Kolb, 1984)
2. ✓ **Motor responses and kinesthetic experience** (Bruner, 1966)
3. ✓ **Direct manipulation** of materials, tools, or objects (Montessori, 1917; Fleming, 1992)
4. ✓ **Concrete experience** as foundation for learning (Kolb, 1984)
5. ✓ **Learning by doing** vs. passive observation (Dewey, 1938)

### Critical Question: Assembly vs. Operation

**Does "operating/controlling a robot" count as hands-on according to foundational theory?**

| Theorist | Building/Assembly | Operating/Controlling |
|----------|------------------|----------------------|
| **Dewey (1938)** | ✓ Active engagement, problem-solving | ✓ Active engagement, problem-solving with tool |
| **Kolb (1984)** | ✓ Concrete Experience (Stage 1) | ✓ Concrete Experience (Stage 1) |
| **Bruner (1966)** | ✓ Enactive mode (motor responses) | ✓ Enactive mode (motor responses) |
| **Fleming (1992)** | ✓ Kinesthetic (building, crafting) | ✓ Kinesthetic (using tools, equipment) |
| **Montessori (1917)** | ✓ Manipulative (assembling puzzles) | ✓ Manipulative (using tools, pouring) |

### Answer: YES, both are hands-on

**Theoretical Justification:**

1. **Dewey**: Emphasized **problem-solving activity** - using tools to accomplish tasks qualifies as "learning by doing"

2. **Kolb**: Stage 1 = "Concrete Experience" defined as **actively doing** something, not restricted to building/construction

3. **Bruner**: "Enactive mode" = **motor responses and physical actions** - operating/controlling involves motor activity

4. **Fleming**: Kinesthetic explicitly includes "**using tools and equipment**" - not just building them

5. **Montessori**: Used manipulatives that children **operated/used** (pouring, fastening), not only constructed

## Implications for BuildBots Study

### Both Groups Involve Hands-On Learning

**Experimental Group (Assembly):**
- **Type of hands-on**: Enactive engagement with **robot structure**
- Physical manipulation of **components** (parts, screws, joints)
- Motor responses: fine motor skills for assembly
- Concrete experience: understanding HOW robot is built

**Control Group (Operation):**
- **Type of hands-on**: Enactive engagement with **robot function**
- Physical manipulation of **controls** (buttons, interfaces, movements)
- Motor responses: coordinating robot movements
- Concrete experience: understanding WHAT robot can do

### Key Distinction:

**Not** hands-on vs. non-hands-on  
**Rather**: Two types of hands-on engagement

- **Hands-on with STRUCTURE** (assembly/construction)
- **Hands-on with FUNCTION** (operation/control)

### What Makes Assembly "Different" from Operation?

According to theory, the distinction is:

1. **Level of internal exposure** (Hitron et al., 2019 "black box" concept)
   - Assembly: exposes internal mechanisms
   - Operation: internal mechanisms remain hidden

2. **Type of motor engagement** (Bruner's enactive mode)
   - Assembly: constructing/assembling actions
   - Operation: controlling/directing actions

3. **Problem-solving focus** (Dewey)
   - Assembly: spatial, mechanical problem-solving
   - Operation: functional, task-oriented problem-solving

4. **Ownership effects** (Norton et al., 2012)
   - Assembly: IKEA effect (labor → value)
   - Operation: User effect (familiarity → preference)

### Example Introduction Paragraph:

> "Hands-on learning, rooted in Dewey's (1938) philosophy of 'learning by doing' and formalized through Kolb's (1984) experiential learning cycle, emphasizes active physical engagement with materials and tools as foundational to knowledge construction. Bruner (1966) identified the 'enactive mode' of representation—knowledge stored as motor responses and physical actions—as a critical stage in cognitive development. In the context of educational robotics, hands-on engagement can take multiple forms: learners may physically construct or assemble robots, thereby exposing internal mechanisms and developing technical understanding (Hitron et al., 2019; Williams et al., 2019), or they may operate and control robots, applying motor skills to accomplish tasks and solve problems (Wang et al., 2024). While both forms involve active, kinesthetic engagement consistent with hands-on learning principles (Fleming & Mills, 1992; Montessori, 1917), they differ in the aspect of the robot with which learners engage (structure vs. function) and consequently in the type of understanding they develop."
