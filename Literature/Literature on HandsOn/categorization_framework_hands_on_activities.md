# Categorization Framework: Hands-On Activities with Robots

**Created:** April 9, 2026  
**Purpose:** Framework for distinguishing different types of hands-on engagement with robots

---

## Executive Summary

The literature reveals that "hands-on learning" with robots is **not a monolithic concept** but encompasses multiple distinct types of activities with different cognitive demands, physical engagement levels, and learning outcomes. This document provides a comprehensive framework for categorizing these activities.

---

## 1. Multi-Dimensional Framework

Based on recent literature (particularly Zhong et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2024; Maker Ed resources), hands-on robotics activities can be categorized along multiple dimensions:

### Dimension 1: **Type of Physical Engagement**

#### A. **Creating/Building from Scratch**
**Definition:** Designing and constructing a robot from individual components without predefined plans

**Characteristics:**
- Highest level of creative decision-making
- Must make structural, mechanical, and design choices
- Problem-solving with minimal constraints
- Can fail in fundamental ways (structural collapse, non-functionality)

**Example Activities:**
- Designing a custom robot for a specific task
- Creating novel mechanisms
- Inventing new robot forms

**Cognitive Demands:**
- Design thinking
- Spatial reasoning
- Engineering principles
- Creative problem-solving
- Structural planning

**Learning Outcomes:**
- Deep understanding of mechanical principles
- Strong ownership and attachment
- High self-efficacy (if successful)
- Innovation and creativity skills

---

#### B. **Assembling from Instructions**
**Definition:** Following step-by-step instructions to construct a robot from a kit

**Characteristics:**
- Moderate creative decision-making (choose colors, minor modifications)
- Predefined structure and outcome
- Problem-solving within constraints
- Can fail through incorrect assembly, missing steps

**Example Activities:**
- Building LEGO Mindstorms from instructions
- Assembling Marty robot from manual
- Putting together DIY robot kits

**Cognitive Demands:**
- Following sequential instructions
- Spatial visualization
- Part identification
- Fine motor skills
- Troubleshooting assembly errors

**Learning Outcomes:**
- Understanding of robot structure and components
- Sense of ownership (IKEA effect)
- Sense of accomplishment
- Technical literacy (how parts fit together)
- Reduced "black box" perception

**BuildBots Experimental Group:** This category

---

#### C. **Tinkering/Modifying**
**Definition:** Taking apart, reassembling, modifying, or customizing existing robots

**Characteristics:**
- Exploratory, playful approach
- Iteration and rapid prototyping
- "Failing forward" mentality
- Reverse engineering

**Example Activities:**
- Taking apart a robot to see how it works
- Swapping components between robots
- Customizing appearance or function
- Experimenting with modifications

**Cognitive Demands:**
- Reverse engineering thinking
- Hypothesis testing
- Iterative problem-solving
- Systems thinking

**Learning Outcomes:**
- Deep understanding through exploration
- Comfort with failure and iteration
- Practical troubleshooting skills
- Creative modification skills

---

#### D. **Programming**
**Definition:** Writing code to control robot behavior (with or without physical robot)

**Characteristics:**
- Cognitive rather than kinesthetic
- Abstract thinking about sequences and logic
- Can be done with physical robot or simulator
- Feedback through robot behavior

**Example Activities:**
- Block-based coding (Scratch, Blockly)
- Text-based coding (Python, C++)
- Visual programming interfaces
- Remote control programming

**Cognitive Demands:**
- Algorithmic thinking
- Sequencing and logic
- Debugging
- Abstraction

**Learning Outcomes:**
- Computational thinking
- Programming literacy
- Understanding of robot "intelligence"
- Control and autonomy concepts

**BuildBots Both Groups:** Weeks 4-6

---

#### E. **Interacting/Using**
**Definition:** Using a pre-assembled, pre-programmed robot for social or task-oriented interaction

**Characteristics:**
- Minimal physical manipulation
- Focus on social or functional interaction
- No exposure to internal workings
- User role rather than creator role

**Example Activities:**
- Conversing with social robot
- Learning from robot tutor
- Playing games with robot
- Observing robot demonstrations

**Cognitive Demands:**
- Social cognition
- Task completion
- Communication
- Pattern recognition (of robot behavior)

**Learning Outcomes:**
- Social interaction skills
- Immediate engagement
- May increase anthropomorphism
- Limited technical understanding
- "Black box" perception may persist

**BuildBots Control Group:** Weeks 1-3

---

### Dimension 2: **Teaching/Learning Model** (Zhong et al., 2022)

Orthogonal to physical engagement type:

#### Product-Oriented vs. Knowledge-Oriented
- **Product-Oriented:** Focus on creating functional artifact
- **Knowledge-Oriented:** Focus on understanding concepts

#### Forward vs. Reverse Teaching
- **Forward Teaching:** Start with concepts, apply to project
- **Reverse Teaching:** Start with product, extract concepts

**Four Model Types:**
1. **Project-Based Learning (PBL):** Product-oriented, forward teaching
2. **Reverse Engineering:** Product-oriented, reverse teaching
3. **Scientific Inquiry:** Knowledge-oriented, forward teaching
4. **Troubleshooting/Debugging:** Knowledge-oriented, reverse teaching

---

### Dimension 3: **Engineering Design Process Stage**

#### Five Stages (from Co-Robotics literature):
1. **Design Stage:** Conceptualization, brainstorming, sketching
2. **Production Stage:** Creating components (3D printing, manufacturing)
3. **Assembly Stage:** Putting components together
4. **Programming Stage:** Adding control and intelligence
5. **Test & Validation Stage:** Debugging, iterating, improving

**Note:** Different hands-on activities engage different stages

---

### Dimension 4: **Technical Domain** (Core Areas)

#### Three Domains:
1. **Building & Mechanics**
   - Structural integrity
   - Gears, motors, wheels
   - Physical construction

2. **Electronics & Sensors**
   - Circuits and wiring
   - Sensors (proximity, light, touch)
   - Power systems

3. **Programming & Control**
   - Code writing
   - Algorithmic logic
   - Behavior design

**Note:** Activities can span multiple domains

---

### Dimension 5: **Level of Autonomy** (What the Robot Does)

Different from what the learner does:

1. **Teleoperated:** Direct human control
2. **Semi-Autonomous:** Follows programmed sequences
3. **Autonomous:** Makes independent decisions
4. **Adaptive:** Learns and changes behavior

**Note:** Affects learning about AI concepts

## 3. Psychological Mechanisms by Activity Type

### Creating from Scratch
**Mechanisms:**
- Maximum sense of ownership (created from nothing)
- Maximum sense of accomplishment
- High self-efficacy (if successful)
- Identity expression through design
- Deepest understanding of functionality

**Potential Risks:**
- High frustration if unsuccessful
- May not complete project
- Requires substantial prior knowledge

---

### Assembling from Instructions
**Mechanisms:**
- IKEA effect (effort → value)
- Sense of accomplishment (completed artifact)
- Moderate self-efficacy
- Demystification (seeing how it's built)
- Moderate ownership

**Potential Risks:**
- Frustration with complex instructions
- Errors in assembly → need troubleshooting
- Less creative expression

**BuildBots Experimental Group**

---

### Tinkering/Modifying
**Mechanisms:**
- Discovery learning
- Comfort with failure
- Playful exploration
- Hypothesis testing
- Deep technical understanding through experimentation

**Potential Risks:**
- May break robot
- Needs "safe to fail" environment
- Can be unfocused without guidance

---

### Programming
**Mechanisms:**
- Abstract thinking
- Immediate feedback (robot behavior)
- Debugging as learning
- Understanding of robot "intelligence"

**Potential Risks:**
- Can be frustrating (syntax errors)
- May remain abstract without physical robot
- Less ownership than physical building

---

### Interacting
**Mechanisms:**
- Social engagement
- Immediate gratification
- Low barrier to entry
- May develop attachment through interaction

**Potential Risks:**
- Limited technical understanding
- May increase unrealistic expectations
- "Black box" thinking persists
- Anthropomorphism may increase

**BuildBots Control Group (Weeks 1-3)**

## 4. Implications for BuildBots Study

### What BuildBots Actually Tests:

**Experimental Group (Building Group):**
- Actually: **Assembling from Instructions** (not building from scratch)
- Weeks 1-3: Assembly activity
- Weeks 4-6: Programming + Interaction

**Control Group (Interaction Group):**
- **Interacting with Pre-Built Robot**
- Weeks 1-3: Interaction only
- Weeks 4-6: Programming + Interaction (same as experimental)

### Key Contrast:
**Assembly vs. Interaction** during Weeks 1-3
- Both groups do programming in Weeks 4-6
- Difference is in initial exposure: hands-on assembly vs. social interaction

### Predictions Based on Categorization:

**Assembly (Experimental) Should Lead To:**
1. **Higher technical understanding** - exposed to internal structure
2. **Higher ownership** - IKEA effect, effort invested
3. **Higher attachment** - ownership leads to attachment
4. **Lower anthropomorphism** - see it as machine, not just social agent
5. **More realistic perception** - understand capabilities and limitations
6. **Higher self-efficacy** - accomplished assembly task

**Interaction (Control) Should Lead To:**
1. **Lower technical understanding** - black box persists
2. **Lower ownership** - not involved in creation
3. **Lower attachment** - no creation effort
4. **Higher anthropomorphism** - focus on social features
5. **Less realistic perception** - may overestimate capabilities
6. **Higher immediate engagement** - social interaction is immediately rewarding

## 5. Literature Support for Categories

### Creating vs. Assembling Distinction:
- Technical robotics literature (MIT assembler robots, 2022)
- Architectural robotics (building = generative, assembly = aggregation)
- **Gap:** Educational literature doesn't clearly distinguish

### Assembly → Ownership:
- Norton et al. (2012) - IKEA effect
- Franke et al. (2010) - "I designed it myself"
- Sun & Sundar (2016) - Self-assembly affects user experience
- IKEA robots (2024) - Assembly strengthens relationships
- Huang et al. (2013) - Attachment from building LEGO robots
- Groom et al. (2009) - Self-built vs. other-built comparison

### Hands-On → Technical Understanding:
- Hitron et al. (2019) - Reduces black box thinking
- Williams et al. (2019) - Building supports AI concept understanding
- Embodied cognition literature - Physical manipulation aids understanding

### Interaction → Anthropomorphism:
- Williams et al. (2019) - Children with lower knowledge see robots as more human-like
- Social robot literature - Interaction increases social perception
- **Prediction:** Building may counter this by emphasizing mechanical nature

## 6. Recommendations for Future Research

### Need for Standardized Taxonomy:
- **Current issue:** "Building," "assembling," "making," "tinkering" used inconsistently
- **Solution:** Adopt standardized terminology with clear definitions
- **Proposed:** Use framework from this document as starting point

### Need for Comparative Studies:
- Most studies examine ONE type of activity
- Need direct comparisons: Creating vs. Assembling vs. Tinkering vs. Programming vs. Interacting
- BuildBots contributes: Assembling vs. Interacting comparison

### Need for Mechanism Studies:
- **Question:** What specific mechanisms drive learning in each activity type?
- Is it time on task? Ownership? Physical engagement? Problem-solving?
- Need studies that measure potential mediators

### Need for Age/Development Studies:
- Optimal activity type may vary by age
- Young children: may need more structured (assembly) before open-ended (creating)
- Older children/adolescents: may benefit more from creating and tinkering
- Need developmental perspective

## 7. Practical Implications for Educators

### Choosing Activity Type:

**Use Creating/Building when:**
- Goal: Innovation, creativity, design thinking
- Students have prior experience with assembly
- Time and resources available for extended projects
- Failure is acceptable/encouraged

**Use Assembling when:**
- Goal: Understanding structure and function
- Introduction to robotics
- Limited time or resources
- Need structured learning experience
- Want to ensure successful completion

**Use Tinkering when:**
- Goal: Deep exploration and discovery
- Want to encourage experimentation
- Have "safe to fail" environment
- Students need to develop troubleshooting skills

**Use Programming when:**
- Goal: Computational thinking and coding skills
- Physical robot may or may not be available
- Focus on behavior rather than structure

**Use Interaction when:**
- Goal: Social learning, communication skills
- Introduction to robots (build interest)
- Physical limitations prevent building
- Focus on human-robot interaction concepts

### Combining Activities:

**Recommended Sequence:**
1. **Interaction** (brief) → Generate interest
2. **Assembly** → Understand structure
3. **Programming** → Add behavior
4. **Tinkering** → Modify and explore
5. **Creating** → Apply knowledge to novel design

**BuildBots Sequence:**
- Experimental: Assembly → Programming → Interaction
- Control: Interaction → Programming → More Interaction

## 8. Key Takeaways for Paper 4

1. **"Building" is ambiguous** - Need to specify: Creating, Assembling, or Tinkering

2. **BuildBots is Assembly study** - Not creation from scratch, but following instructions

3. **Assembly ≠ Interaction** - Fundamentally different engagement types
   - Assembly: Physical, technical, hands-on
   - Interaction: Social, functional, hands-off

4. **Multiple mechanisms** - Not just one way that "hands-on" works:
   - Ownership effect (IKEA)
   - Demystification (Hitron)
   - Embodied learning (tangible computing)
   - Problem-solving with feedback (Huang)

5. **Context matters** - Same activity (e.g., programming) different outcomes with vs. without physical robot

6. **Need clear definitions** - Paper 4 should clearly define "building group" as "assembly group"
