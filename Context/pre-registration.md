Description
Research on social robots in education has received significant interest over the past two decades, with a variety of different roles and different robots studied. Prior work empathizes the positive effects of social robots on children’s affective and cognitive learning outcomes in application areas such as second-language learning, literacy, and engineering with robots taking on roles such as teachers, tutors, and peers. At the same time, robots are still rarely seen in classrooms, libraries, or homes. Reasons for this include (i) logistical and technical challenges associated with deploying autonomous, yet effective social robots, (ii) purchase and deployment costs opposed to limited school budgets, (iii) a lack of long-term engagement due to missing adaptivity and customization of current systems, and (iv) unrealistic expectations of stakeholders regarding robot capabilities. Instead of deploying costly, complex, and ready-to-use social robots, we propose a hands-on approach that engages children in both constructing social robots and interacting with them. By including children in the building process of low complexity Do-it-Yourself (DIY) robot kits, teachers may foster children’s AI literacy and equip them with the necessary knowledge to create AI artifacts that they would understand technically.   We will deploy a 6-weeks ‘build-and-learn’ curriculum, centered around the social robot Marty. In weeks 1-3, children will either assemble their own robots (experimental group) or interact with a preassembled robot (control group). In weeks 4-6, both groups will receive theoretical and practical lessons around the assembled robot, programming, and AI and LLMs. We will conduct pre, mid-, and post-tests of children’s engagement, subjective and objective learning outcomes (RQ1), as well as their perception of and attachment with robots (RQ2-RQ3). Additionally, we will assess the practicality of the lessons after each session from the perspectives of teachers and students using qualitative assessments.

We motivate these hypotheses based on the following literature review:
Hands-on learning has been around since Papert’s constructionism (1980) and Piaget’s constructivism (1975; for a review see Ackermann, 2004). Since then, various research has highlighted the positive effects of hands-on learning on learning outcomes across ages and contexts (Gil, 2016; Hitron et al., 2019; Williams et al., 2019; Druga et al., 2019; Huang et al., 2013; Hitron et al., 2019). For example, in a 2014 meta-analysis, hands-on learning has been found to improve student performance in science, engineering, and mathematics compared to traditional lecturing (Freeman et al., 2014). Especially for children, for whom sitting and listening for an extend period of time can be difficult, hands-on learning proves to be beneficial (Diamond et al., 2019). In the context of AI, Hitron and colleagues showed that learning through experience can support children’s understanding of basic machine learning concepts and create a better understanding of AI. Similarly, Williams and colleagues (2019) engaged children in a hands-on robot building and programming activity and assessed children’s AI knowledge. The authors found that their building activity supported children well in grasping AI concepts. Additionally, in a study by Huang and colleagues (2013), robot-building created problem-solving situations with direct feedback, which was associated with increased self-efficacy and interest to work with a robot (Huang et al., 2013). Adult participants in their sample described the building activity as a creative process rather than a duty, which in turn motivated them to spend more effort on the interaction (Huang et al., 2013). This increased engagement and effort may in turn increase students’ learning gains and learning retention. 

In line with the reviewed literature, we thus expect robot-building to be associated with higher engagement over time (H1) and higher learning gains across subjective (H2) and objective learning outcomes (H4-6), compared to only interacting with a robot.

RQ2-3: Robot perception, attachment, and intention to use
In 2012, Norton and colleagues (2012) introduced the IKEA effect, indicating that participants report higher satisfaction with purchased IKEA furniture when they assembled this furniture themselves. Likewise, in 2009 Franke and colleagues (2009) proposed the “I designed it Myself” effect, in which participants perceived higher value of products solely based on having the feeling of being the creator of it. Both affects are mediated by sense of ownership and feelings of accomplishment (Franke et al., 2009; Sun & Sundar, 2025). In human-robot interaction, multiple studies have since investigated the effects of robot-assembly with adults and found similar results. For example, Groom and colleagues (2009) report lower maliciousness ratings, higher friendliness ratings, self-extension, attachment, and team feelings with a self-build robot versus an other-assembled robot. In Sun and Sundar’s (2025) work, participants evaluated both the robot and the robot interaction as more positive when assembling the robot themselves and this effect was mediated by sense of ownership and sense of accomplishment. Additionally, in work by Huang et al. (2013) participants that engaged in a robot building activity reported feelings of higher effort, self-efficacy, and personal engagement. A majority of participants in their study additionally reported increased attachment and negative feelings when being asked to dismantle the robot, due to for example the time and effort put into building the robot. Attachment has also been linked to increased intention to use (Zhang & Yu, 2025), thus possibly increasing long-term engagement and contributing to successful robot deployment. Fewer studies have investigated the effect of robot-building on children’s perception. Williams and colleagues (2019) investigated how a robot building activity affected pre-schoolers’ (4-6-years) perceptions of a robots’ theory of mind, agency, intelligence, and socialness. From pre- to post-test, children’s perception of robots’ ability to learn and being more like adults than children increased. Additionally, children’s perception of robots as smart and human-like was dependent on their AI knowledge: Children with higher AI knowledge were more likely to see robots as people rather than toys, and disagreed less with robots being smarter than them. 

In line with the reviewed work, we expect children in the build group to perceive the robot as warmer (H9) and less discomforting (H10) over time. We additionally expect children in the build group to feel more attached (H13) and willing to use the robot (H14) compared to children in the interaction group. We may also expect children in the build group to perceive their robots as more competent and anthropomorphic. However, we expect two counteracting mechanisms to be at play, that may result in lower ratings of these concepts, but a higher safety rating in the building group compared to the interaction group. First, children in the building group will be exposed to the technical features of the robot and may develop a more informed and precise understanding of robots’ capabilities. Hitron and colleagues (2019) investigated children’s understanding of machine learning concepts, by allowing children to experience and uncover machine learning building blocks. The authors show that children’s hands-on learning experiences contributed to a better understanding of machine learning concepts. Second, children in the interaction group will spend more time in social interactions with the robot, which may conflate their perceptions of robot anthropomorphism. Paired with children’s enhanced understanding of a robot as a machine and their reduced “black box” thinking (Hitron et al., 2019), this may increase safety perceptions (H11), but decrease perceptions of the robot as a social entity and consequently reduce perceptions of competence (H7) and anthropomorphism (H12). However, these effects may be moderated by children’s prior knowledge (Williams and colleagues, 2019).

Design Plan

Study type

Experiment - A researcher randomly assigns treatments to study subjects, this includes field or lab experiments. This is also known as an intervention experiment and includes randomized controlled trials.
Blinding

No blinding is involved in this study.
Is there any additional blinding in this study?

Children will not be actively aware that there are two conditions and which condition they have been assigned to. However, we cannot rule out that children will discuss parts of the experiment with other students in their school, possibly including members of the other group. We will control for this by asking students in the post-test if and when they have discussed content with others and whom.
Study design 
Updated

We will deploy a quasi-experimental, active control group design with the between subjects factor condition (building vs. interaction). Children will participate in a 6-weeks school curriculum. In the experimental group, triads of children will build a robot in sessions 1-3, while the control group will interact with an assembled robot. Both groups will then receive theoretical lessons paired with robot interaction blocks in sessions 4-6. We will conduct within subjects pre-, mid-, post- and learning retentions tests, as well as weekly assessments after each session for students and teachers.

LLM interaction framework:
We programmed a new open-source method for LLM interaction design, called Rodin's Four-Slot Frame Engine, that assesses the user’s input, shapes and generates a draft response, checks its validity and regenerates a repaired answer if needed. The Rodin architecture provides a robust, modular, and efficient solution by breaking the process of text/speech generation into distinct, composable stages. It consists of adding a modular layer of coding that could work with different LLMs language models and in different contexts, by using external memory and a system of checks at different stages of the generation process. 
Children will interact with this framework in the last week of data collection and will have a 15 Minutes interaction with Marty, in which they will create a mnemonic on microcontrollers.

Randomization

Children will participate in the context of their regular natural sciences, technology and technic class. As such, classes will be randomized to a conditon. Classes 1 and 2 will participate in the experimental condition, while classes 3 and 4 will participate in the control condition.

Data collection procedures 
Updated

The data collection will take place in a rural German comprehensive school as part of the regular natural sciences, technology and technic class. Four classes with two teachers have already been recruited (n = 64). All students in the recruited classes will be included in the data collection, if informed consent was provided by parents and children. 

Data collection will run from November 2025 to ~March 2026, accounting for school holidays. We will start with the experimental groups before Christmas break, and run the control groups after the Christmas break. Each session will last approximately 3 school hours (= 2 1/2 hours). 

Assessments will be performed within the regular sessions. The pre-test will be conducted at the start of session 1. The mid-test will be conducted at the end of session 3. The post-test will be conducted at the end of session 6. Two learning retention tests will be conducted approximately one week and seven weeks after session 6. The weekly assessments will be conducted at the end of sessions 1-6.

Participants will not be monetarily compensated for their participation but will receive a sticker after the session. Teachers will be compensated with a 100€ donation to their classroom funds.

LLM interaction framework:
The developed LLM interaction framework used in the last week will additionally collect data from the 15-minute interaction with children.

Sample size

We will collect data from 64 students, based on the class sizes of the four participating classes. The data structure will be nested within sessions (6), participants (64), and triads (10).
Sample size rationale

The sample size of 64 results from practical considerations, such as the availability of robots (10), and available resources (time, personnel) for data collection. 
Stopping rule

Data collection will conclude after the learning retentions tests have been completed in both conditions.

2. Interaction and Turn-Taking Metrics

- events.turn_count: The sequential count of turns within the conversation.
- events.speaker: Identifies the participant speaking in each turn (e.g., "Red", "Green", "Blue", "assistant").
- events.contribution_type: Categorizes the nature of a student's contribution (e.g., "mnemonic_suggestion", "knowledge_statement", "builds_on_idea", "recall_attempt").
- events.is_relevant: A boolean flag indicating whether a student's contribution was relevant to the topic.
- events.off_topic_duration: Measures the duration (in seconds) that a conversation deviates from the main topic.
- events._suggested_next_speaker: The AI's suggestion for the next speaker, aimed at balancing participation.
- events._consecutive_same_speaker: Tracks consecutive turns by the same speaker.
- events.data.underparticipating_students: A list of students identified as having lower participation.
- frame_memory.balanced_turns_validator.participation.contribution_count: The total number of contributions 
- frame_memory.balanced_turns_validator.participation.total_speaking_time_seconds: The cumulative speaking time for each student.
- frame_memory.balanced_turns_validator.participation.last_contribution_time: Timestamp of each student's most recent contribution.

3. Content and Comprehension

- events.data.message` / `events.data.user: The actual textual content of student inputs.
- events.data.assistant: The textual responses provided by the AI assistant.
- events.data.mnemonic_progress: A narrative summary of the progress in developing the mnemonic device.
- events.data.summary: A brief summary of the student's message in each turn.
- frame_memory.comprehension_tracker.concepts: A comprehensive list of all concepts discussed and tracked throughout the session.
- events.data.per_turn_analysis.understood: Concepts that were identified as understood by students in a particular turn.
- events.data.per_turn_analysis.confused: Concepts that were identified as confusing for students in a particular turn.
- events.data.cumulative_assessments_updated.concept: The specific concept being evaluated.
- events.data.cumulative_assessments_updated.level: The assessed comprehension level of a concept (e.g., "UNDERSTOOD").
- events.data.cumulative_assessments_updated.justification: The reasoning or evidence supporting the assigned comprehension level.

4. Mnemonic Creation Process

- events.session_phase: Indicates the current stage of the session (e.g., Phase 1: concept generation, Phase 2: mnemonic construction, Phase 3: recall).
- frame_memory.mnemonic_state.selected_concepts: The set of concepts chosen for inclusion in the mnemonic device.
- frame_memory.mnemonic_state.concepts_finalized: A boolean indicating whether the selection of concepts for the mnemonic has been completed.
- frame_memory.mnemonic_state.mnemonic_text: The final, complete text of the created mnemonic device.
- frame_memory.mnemonic_state.mnemonic_created: A boolean flag confirming the successful creation of the mnemonic.
- frame_memory.mnemonic_state._quality_validated: Indicates if the quality of the mnemonic was formally assessed.
- frame_memory.mnemonic_state._quality_passed: A boolean indicating whether the mnemonic passed the quality assessment.
- frame_memory.mnemonic_state._quality_feedback: Any feedback received regarding the quality of the mnemonic.

5. Recall Performance

- frame_memory.recall_tracking..attempts: The number of times each student attempted to recall the mnemonic.
- frame_memory.recall_tracking..successful_parts: The portions of the mnemonic that each student successfully recalled.
- frame_memory.recall_tracking..stuck_on: The specific parts of the mnemonic where each student encountered difficulties during recall.
- frame_memory.recall_tracking..last_attempt: The content of the last recall attempt made by each student.

6. System Feedback and Validation

- events.data.feedback: Messages generated by the validation frames (e.g., `TURN-TAKING ERROR`, `The response did not adhere to the Phase Goal`).
- events.data.action: The action recommended by a validator (e.g., "pass", "revise").

Post-hoc tests:
We will compute post-hoc tests using emmeans and will apply appropriate corrections for multiple testing.

Convergence and singularity issues:
If the models fail to converge or issue singularity warnings, we will proceed in the following order: 
1) We will follow the pipeline as described by Scandola and Tidoni (2021). 
2) The allFit command from the lme4 package (Bates et al., 2015) which includes optimizers from the packages dfoptim (Varadhan et al., 2020) and optimx (Nash & Varadhan, 2011) will be used to check if some optimisers will not issue warnings. 
3) If the models fail to converge or issue singularity warnings after step 1 and 2, we will instead conduct the analysis using a Bayesian model, using the brm function from the brms package (Bürkner, 2017; 2018). The model specifications will stay the same and we will use uninformative priors. 

Additionally, we will assess the practicalities of running the program in open-ended questions to students and teachers and interviews with teachers. This data will be explored with a qualitative analyses.

Inference criteria

Statistical significance will be determined based on an alpha level of .05. Appropriate p-value corrections will be applied for multiple testing (e.g., post hoc tests). Two tailed tests will be used.
